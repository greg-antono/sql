### Ethics

Read: Qadri, R. (2021, November 11). _When Databases Get to Define Family._  Wired. <br>

**What values systems are embedded in databases and data systems you encounter in your day-to-day life?**

Consider, for example, concepts of fariness, inequality, social structures, marginalization, intersection of technology and society, etc.


```
I grew up in Singapore where, much like what was discussed about Pakistan’s family design and digital database issues, much of everyone’s identities are tied to values in a database. Here’s a picture to illustrate this: every Singapore citizen has an identity card that states full name, name in their mother tongue, their personal identity number, their ethnicity, their thumbprint, and their home address. In a pursuit to digitize almost everything, the government has also developed an application called SingPass, which consolidates information like those on the identity card, but also with other databases, including marriage records, health records, grades from national exams (even if it was from years and years ago!), educational certificates and diplomas, and so on. While this makes the application a one-stop shop for one’s personal information (for the user), it goes on to remind me that all of this information also belongs to the government.

Of course, not everything can fit nicely into neat categories or bins. Unlike health records and examination scores, one’s ethnic, gender and sexuality identities do not necessarily fit into these rigid or arbitrary categories. Take for instance Singapore’s CMIO model: the Singaporean population is classified into four ethnic groups: Chinese, Malay, Indian and Other – an effort to promote multiculturalism and equal representation in the development of policies (such as housing and employment). For instance, local apartment buildings typically has a quota and breakdown for how many units should be occupied by Chinese/Malay/Indian families. While this strategy might have been implemented in the early days as a way to integrate and balance a diverse population, these categories are ultimately arbitrary and oversimplify the complex identities that people have. “Chinese” lumps together speakers of Cantonese, Hokkien, or any other dialect group, as does “Indian” for groups who might speak Tamil or Hindi. The “Other” category is even more confusing, as essentially an “elsewhere” condition. This classification has proven difficult for mixed-race families looking for housing: if a Malay and Chinese couple apply for housing, would they be lumped under the Chinese bin (i.e., access to more housing units?), or will they be disadvantaged if lumped under the Malay bin (where there are fewer units)? I’ve also seen mixed-race Singaporeans who face similar uneasiness with these categories thrust upon them.

Very similarly to what was briefly discussed in the article, the values mapped to Singaporean individuals in these databases also fail to do justice for folks who do not map neatly into standards of what a heteronormative, traditional nuclear family looks like. Access to subsidized housing is contingent on one’s marital status: a married couple can apply for housing at any point, but single individuals have to wait until they turn 35. Single parents face troubles having access to these housing opportunities as well. Unsurprisingly, members of the LGBTQIA+ community face great challenges too, since housing benefits are only granted to legally recognized, heterosexual marriages (i.e., reflected and approved in their databases). As a queer Singaporean, I am personally fully aware that even if I got married to my partner in Canada, the value in my Marriage Status column in their database will still remain as “Single”.

It’s often curious to think about how these national databases (Pakistan’s, as described in the article); and Singapore’s) contain so much information about individuals and their identities and behaviours, but a lot of it depends on the labels and categories that powers that be created to begin with: the homogenization and dilution of ethnicities on our Singaporean identity cards, the complete erasure and failure to recognize queer folks in official capacities. All these reflect governmental decisions for policy planning, and the preservation of “traditional values”, can distinctly be seen in the labels issued to citizens.
```
